There are a few things I want the lawyer taking this case to understand about me and about why I am here.

I do not need anyone to agree with my theory of the case. I do not want the hospital or any doctor judged according to rules I have invented. I want to know what rules actually governed them, and I want what happened to me tested against those rules. If what happened was acceptable by those standards, I will accept that. If it was not, I want those responsible to account for it.

I also want it understood that I remember the beginning and the end of my 2017 admission very clearly.

On Day 2, after the complex case meeting, my neurologist came to my bedside. I remember him telling me that his senior had told him not to use the “good stuff” — levodopa — and that in time we might regret that decision. When I asked what he meant by regret, I remember him telling me that he did not want me coming back in seven or eight years saying that my life could have been so much better.

That was effectively how our clinical relationship began.

On Day 4, my feet were being directed towards Podiatry. But this was not a new world to me. The neurologist himself recorded that I had been receiving chiropody and podiatry treatment for the foot problem since 2009–10. I knew from years of experience what that treatment could do for me and what remained unresolved. At the same time, the neurological relationship to the feet had not been established; it remained “Not clear”.

Then came Day 8.

With discharge approaching, I remember challenging the neurologist because the problem with my feet had still not been explained. I remember the medical students. I remember one of them going to get him. I remember trying to explain the problem using the NCT and bald-tyres analogy.

That conversation is absent from the extensive account of my admission.

So there is something very simple that I want understood before litigation begins:

I remember what the neurologist said to me near the beginning of the admission, and I remember what I said to him near the end. The substance of the first conversation found its way into his account. My challenge at the end did not.

I then lived with the consequences of whatever happened clinically and administratively after that admission.

Over the following years, the record accumulated evidence that my neurological state and neurological treatment could affect what was happening in my feet. Yet the foot problem continued. By 2023 the clinical record was describing the pain as causing a “death wish”. By 2025 my neurologist was recording me saying, in the context of the feet, “I won't live with it”.

The complete record contains no safeguarding response.

That is the point at which my own decision became settled.

I am taking the case because I believe something that began as an unresolved clinical problem was allowed to continue until the suffering associated with that same problem entered language about whether I could continue living with it.

That is my reason for taking the case.

It is not proof of negligence. It is not the standard of care. It is not an instruction to an expert about what conclusion to reach.

What I want now is much simpler.

Take the record. Identify the rules that governed the people responsible. Test what happened against those rules. And tell me what follows.
