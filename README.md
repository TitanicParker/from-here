 A litigation handover and working evidential resource

This repository marks a change in the purpose of my work on this case.

Since March 2025 I have been trying to understand the clinical and documentary record: what happened, what was decided, what was left unresolved, what later evidence showed, and how the account of the case developed over time.

On **21 August 2026**, I sent the case back to Daly Hempenstall Solicitors LLP seeking urgent advice on limitation, independent neurological evidence, preservation of evidence and whether protective proceedings or other immediate steps are required.

That email is the line in the sand.

Everything before it was primarily an attempt to reconstruct and understand the case.

**From here, I want the case independently tested and, if sustainable, taken forward.**

---

## Why I am taking the case

My reason for taking the case is personal, but I do not confuse that reason with the legal standard.

The problem with my feet existed for many years before I was first seen by Neurology in July 2017.

The feet were not merely one symptom among many.

They formed part of the presenting problem. The GP record joined gait, feet, muscle tension, movement initiation, tone, dexterity and functional deterioration in the clinical question that brought me into neurological assessment.

Neurology identified a very significant Parkinsonian disorder. It also documented substantial structural abnormalities of the feet.

But the relationship between the neurological condition and the leg/foot symptoms was left **“Not clear”** while a Podiatry pathway became operational.

Immediately before discharge, I recall expressly challenging whether the problem that had brought me into hospital had actually been resolved sufficiently to justify discharge.

That challenge is not identifiable in the extensive patient-facing discharge account.

For me, the omission is not merely one omission among many, just as the feet were not merely one symptom among many.

The feet concerned the adequacy of the clinical explanation.

The protest concerned the adequacy of closure.

---

## Why the later record changed this for me

For years the case could still be approached as an argument about difficult clinical judgment, diagnostic uncertainty and treatment strategy.

The later record changed that.

By **March 2023**, an urgent GP referral documented severe bilateral burning foot and lower-leg pain in language including **“causing death wish”**. The GP expressly recorded concern and sought intervention before the situation deteriorated further.

A contemporaneous Parkinson's nurse communication described the bilateral foot and leg pain as debilitating and recorded an absence of quality of life.

By **March 2025**, the treating neurologist himself documented the severity of the morning foot pain in extreme terms and recorded the patient's expression **“I won't live with it”** as a way of describing the situation.

The same clinical record also acknowledges that Parkinson's medication relieved the experience in the feet.

Earlier records had already shown neurological treatment materially altering the lower-limb and foot state, including deterioration involving the feet during withdrawal of procyclidine. Later records described pain and shuffling in relation to OFF medication state.

I therefore reached a point at which I could no longer regard this simply as an historical disagreement about the correct interpretation of foot symptoms.

The complete record contains repeated crisis-level language attached to the longstanding foot-pain problem.

**No corresponding safeguarding response has been identified in the complete record.**

That is why I have decided that the case should now be independently tested.

It is my reason for taking the case.

It is not, by itself, the legal test.

# Their rules, their record, their explanation

I am not asking a solicitor, expert or court to adopt my personal standards of good medical care.

I do not want the defendants judged against my hopes, expectations or retrospective understanding.

I want them judged against the standards that actually governed them.

If the applicable professional and institutional standards permitted an extensive patient-facing discharge account to preserve my understanding of the plan while omitting a material challenge to its sufficiency, then that can be established.

If those standards permitted the unresolved foot/neurological relationship to remain as it did despite subsequent patient-specific evidence of neurological modifiability, that too can be established.

If the applicable standards required no identifiable safeguarding response when the clinical record described severe pain as causing a death wish, and again later recorded “I won't live with it”, then that can be established.

If the care complied with the relevant standards, I will accept that.

If it did not, I want the departure identified and accounted for.

The purpose of this repository is therefore not to prescribe the answer.

It is to make the underlying questions unusually easy to test against:

- the professional standards applicable to the clinicians;
- the institutional standards applicable to the hospital;
- the standards governing clinical documentation;
- the standards governing patient participation and communication;
- the standards governing the assessment and management of risk;
- and the ordinary legal requirements of breach, causation and damage.

# The central documentary problem

The July 2017 discharge correspondence is unusually extensive.

Its author explained that it was intended in part for the patient, to consolidate discussions, assist with debriefing and provide a clear plan.

The document describes the patient as highly intelligent and insightful, able to carry information forward from day to day and to question very appropriately.

It also records that the patient had a **“good understanding”** of why levodopa or dopamine-agonist treatment was being delayed.

I do not dispute that I understood what was being explained to me.

The question is what happened when I used that understanding.

My account is that, immediately before discharge, I challenged the treating neurologist because the disabling problem involving my feet remained unresolved.

Four medical students were present. I recall one of them going to obtain the consultant. I used a distinctive NCT/bald-tyres analogy to explain the problem: identifying one serious problem did not establish that the problem which had brought me there had been adequately dealt with.

That reported challenge is absent from the final patient-facing account.

The important distinction is therefore:

> **The record preserves the patient's capacity to understand and question, but not the reported question that challenged the sufficiency of closure.**

The significance of that omission is not to be assumed.

It is to be investigated.

# Day 2 / Day 4 / Day 8

One way of understanding the July 2017 admission is through three decision points.

## Day 2 — treatment policy

A cautious treatment strategy developed.

The clinicians contemplated that levodopa might work very well but wished to delay its introduction because of the patient's exceptional youth and the potential long-term consequences of early dopaminergic treatment.

That was a clinical decision capable of expert evaluation.

## Day 4 — operative separation

The relationship between the neurological disorder and the foot/leg symptoms remained uncertain.

The eventual account described it as **“Not clear.”**

Nevertheless, the practical Podiatry pathway was already being established.

This creates an important distinction:

> **The evidential relationship remained unresolved while the management pathways became operationally separated.**

That separation may have been reasonable.

Whether it should subsequently have been reconsidered is a different question.

## Day 8 — challenge to closure

My account is that, with discharge imminent, I brought the unresolved feet back to the treating neurologist and challenged whether the admission had achieved enough to justify ending it.

The importance of this event is not that it proves what the correct medical answer was.

It is that, if independently established, it shows that the sufficiency of closure itself was expressly put in issue.

Without the event, the documentary sequence appears approximately as:

> investigation → uncertainty → plan → understanding → discharge

With it restored:

> investigation → unresolved relationship → management plan → challenge to sufficiency → decision fork → discharge

What potentially disappears from the documentary account is therefore not merely a conversation.

**It is the fact that there was still a choice.**

# Backstop / corridor / backstop

The longer history can be viewed at a different scale.

## Backstop 1 — 2017

The extensive 2017 account establishes the foundational synthesis.

Parkinsonism is strongly identified.

The exact form remains unusually uncertain.

Genetic Parkinsonism, including PRKN/PARK2, is seriously considered.

The foot/leg relationship remains unresolved.

Podiatry is the operative structural-foot route.

Dopaminergic restraint becomes a governing treatment strategy.

The patient is represented as understanding that strategy.

The reported challenge to whether this was sufficient for discharge is absent.

## The corridor — 2017 to 2025

The later record contains repeated information capable of testing parts of that original architecture.

It includes, among other matters:

- profound improvement with neurological treatment;
- deterioration involving the feet during procyclidine withdrawal;
- the treating neurologist's later documentation that the outside-left-foot phenomenon returned rapidly during that withdrawal;
- later dopaminergic treatment and genuine treatment toxicity;
- later descriptions of foot/leg pain and shuffling in relation to medication state;
- severe bilateral foot pain;
- later recognition of OFF-related pain;
- and profound relief following extended overnight Sinemet treatment.

Not every later event proves that the original management was wrong.

Some later evidence strongly supports the original concern about treatment toxicity.

The important question is narrower:

> **When later patient-specific evidence emerged, was the foundational relationship between the neurological disorder and the longstanding foot problem independently reconstructed, or was later care predominantly adapted within the architecture already established?**

That is an expert question.

## Backstop 2 — 2025

In responding to my complaint in 2025, the treating neurologist relied substantially upon the longitudinal correspondence.

He explained that the long letters were intended to provide clear documentation of how decisions had been made, when and by whom, and returned expressly to the 2017 statement that I had a “good understanding” of the treatment rationale.

This gives rise to an important evidential question:

> **Can the documentary history safely be relied upon as demonstrating adequate consideration and communication of the original problem if a material patient challenge to the sufficiency of the discharge plan was omitted from the foundational account?**

Again, the answer is not assumed here.

The question is preserved for independent assessment.

# The omission is central, but it is not the entire legal case

I do not suggest that the absence of one conversation from a medical record automatically establishes negligence.

Nor do I suggest that eight years of harm can simply be attributed to a missing sentence.

The potential clinical case is broader and requires appropriate expert evidence.

A more precise question is whether reasonable neurological care required the unresolved foot/neurological relationship to be integrated or reconsidered:

- at discharge in July 2017;
- when the adequacy of closure was challenged;
- when neurological treatment subsequently affected the feet;
- when later medication-state evidence accumulated;
- and when the foot pain eventually reached crisis-level severity.

The documentary omission matters because it may have removed from the authoritative account evidence that the adequacy of closure had itself been disputed.

Its legal significance is for the lawyers and experts to determine.

The safeguarding issue should also be approached in the same disciplined way.

The complete record contains:

- express GP concern;
- a contemporaneous nursing description of debilitating pain and no quality of life;
- and a 2025 treating-neurologist record using the patient's statement **“I won't live with it”** to describe the severity of the continuing foot-pain problem.

No corresponding safeguarding response has been identified in the complete record.

I am not asking this repository to decide what the governing standard required.

I am asking that the relevant standard be identified.

The appropriate questions include:

> What risk-assessment or safeguarding duties applied in these circumstances?

> What response would reasonably have been expected?

> What does the surviving clinical record show was actually done?

> If no response is documented, what significance should properly be attached to that absence?

> Did any departure cause or materially contribute to subsequent harm?

Those are matters for appropriate professional and legal evidence.

# Evidential discipline

A few rules govern this repository.

## 1. Primary evidence comes first

Where a proposition can be established by a contemporaneous clinical document, correspondence or other primary source, that source should be preferred.

## 2. Patient recollection remains identified as such

My account of the July 2017 bedside protest is detailed and specific.

It should not be converted into institutional fact merely because I believe it occurred.

The appropriate question is whether independent witness or institutional evidence can test it.

Equally, the absence of the event from the very record alleged to be incomplete cannot by itself establish that it did not occur.

## 3. Later evidence is not automatically hindsight proof

Later medication response, OFF phenomena and genetic confirmation may illuminate earlier events.

They do not automatically establish what a reasonable neurologist ought to have known in 2017.

That requires expert evidence.

## 4. Counter-evidence belongs in the case

There was genuine structural foot disease.

There were genuine treatment risks.

Dopaminergic treatment later produced significant adverse effects.

The original clinical decisions should therefore be tested in their actual context rather than reconstructed as obviously unreasonable with hindsight.

## 5. Regulatory proceedings are separate

There are overlapping data-protection and other regulatory matters concerning the documentary record.

They may produce relevant evidence.

They do not determine the clinical-negligence case.

The civil case should not depend upon waiting for a regulatory outcome where limitation may be running.

# What I am asking of the legal team

At this point I am not asking the legal team to adopt the propositions in this repository.

I am asking for independent legal and expert assessment of them.

In particular:

1. **Limitation**
   - determine the applicable limitation periods;
   - determine the legally relevant date or dates of knowledge;
   - and advise whether protective proceedings or other immediate steps are required.

2. **Clinical standard of care**
   - identify the appropriate neurological expertise;
   - determine what reasonable care required at the relevant decision points;
   - and distinguish reasonable clinical uncertainty from any actionable failure to investigate, integrate or reconsider.

3. **Causation**
   - determine whether any identified departure probably caused or materially contributed to prolonged pain, disability or other loss.

4. **Documentation**
   - identify the standards governing the discharge correspondence and patient-facing clinical record;
   - and determine the significance, if any, of the reported protest being absent.

5. **Safeguarding**
   - identify the standards applicable to the 2023 and 2025 crisis-level statements;
   - establish what response was required;
   - and compare that requirement with the complete record.

6. **Evidence preservation**
   - preserve and, where appropriate, obtain drafts, versions and audit history of the July 2017 documentation;
   - investigate the post-discharge DaTscan amendment;
   - identify any surviving evidence capable of reconstructing the bedside teaching encounter and persons present;
   - preserve relevant retention/deletion/audit information;
   - and consider any other evidence likely to become unavailable with further passage of time.

7. **Defendants and causes of action**
   - determine who the proper defendants are;
   - what causes of action are sustainable;
   - and what should not form part of the civil proceedings.
