## The case in three days

The July 2017 admission can be understood through three moments. These are chronology, not a theory imposed on the record.

### Day 2 — treatment policy

A cautious treatment strategy was formed. Ordinary dopaminergic treatment was to be deferred. The surviving account nevertheless records that levodopa might work very well and that, in time, the clinicians might regret not starting it earlier.

The patient remembers the neurologist explaining this at the bedside after the complex case meeting and saying that he did not want the patient returning years later saying that his life could have been so much better.

### Day 4 — the feet acquire a practical destination

The feet were being directed toward Podiatry even though their relationship to the neurological syndrome had not been resolved. The later discharge account described that relationship as **“Not clear.”**

This was not the patient's first experience of chiropody or Podiatry. The neurologist's own history recorded treatment for the foot problem since **2009–10**. The patient therefore says he already understood, from years of experience, what local foot treatment could and could not do for him.

### Day 8 — challenge to discharge

With discharge approaching, the patient recalls challenging the neurologist because the problem with his feet remained unresolved. Four medical students were present. He recalls one of them going to obtain the neurologist and remembers using an NCT / bald-tyres analogy to explain why identifying one serious problem did not establish that the presenting problem had been adequately dealt with.

That account requires independent corroboration.

What is already documentary fact is that the extensive patient-facing discharge account preserves the patient's intelligence, insight, ability to question appropriately and **“good understanding”** of the treatment rationale, while the reported challenge to the sufficiency of discharge is not identifiable in it.

## What happened afterwards

The later record does not support a simple claim that clinicians never reconsidered anything. They did.

It also does not support a simple claim that the feet were wholly neurological. Structural pathology was real, Podiatry treatment was legitimate, pregabalin materially helped pain, and dopaminergic treatment produced genuine toxicity and dyskinesia.

The more difficult longitudinal question is whether accumulating patient-specific evidence required the original relationship between the neurological disorder and the feet to be more explicitly re-examined.

The record includes:

- marked neurological treatment responsiveness in 2017;
- worsening of gait and the feet during procyclidine withdrawal;
- later clinician documentation of rapid return of the outside-left-foot phenomenon during that withdrawal;
- a 2020 foot movement described as **“actually parkinsonian”**;
- the 2021 transition from procyclidine to Sinemet;
- a 2022 morning foot “warm-up” pattern;
- severe bilateral foot/lower-leg pain in 2023;
- later-2023 pain and shuffling described by Neurology as OFF-related / undertreated;
- 2025 recognition that Parkinson's medication relieved the feet and introduction of overnight Sinemet CR;
- later description of **“profound relief”**; and
- 2026 genetic confirmation that the parkinsonism and dystonia were due to compound heterozygous PRKN/Parkin mutations.

The central clinical issue is not whether every later event proves the 2017 management wrong. It is whether, at identifiable points, reasonable care required a fresh integrated assessment of a problem that had remained unresolved at the outset.

## Why I am taking the case

The later record eventually changed the nature of the problem for me.

In **March 2023**, an urgent GP referral recorded severe bilateral burning foot and lower-leg pain as at times extreme and **“causing death wish.”** The GP recorded concern and asked for review **“before we get into trouble here.”** A Parkinson's nurse then recorded debilitating bilateral foot/leg pain and no quality of life.

In **March 2025**, the treating neurologist recorded morning foot pain as **“murder in the feet”**, the first approximately 150 footsteps as **“torture”**, and the patient's formulation **“I won't live with it.”** The same consultation recorded that Parkinson's medication relieved the feelings in the feet.

The complete record contains no safeguarding response corresponding to those crisis-level statements.

That is why my own decision is settled.

It is not, by itself, the legal test.

The legal and expert questions are what the applicable standards required, what the record shows was done, whether any departure occurred, and whether any departure caused or materially contributed to harm.

## Evidential discipline

This repository keeps several propositions separate even where they support one another:

- A positive DaTscan strongly supported Parkinsonism. It did not resolve the cause of every foot symptom.
- Genuine structural foot disease did not exclude neurological modulation of the feet or lower limbs.
- Medication response proves modifiability more readily than it proves mechanism.
- Later PRKN confirmation cannot be treated as knowledge clinicians possessed in 2017.
- Later reconsideration did occur; the question is what was reconsidered and whether the historical problem was ever fully reassembled.
- The patient's bedside challenge remains patient-sourced unless independently corroborated.
- Absence from a record is not automatically proof that an event did not occur or that an omission was deliberate.
- A finding that further assessment should have occurred does not itself establish that a different outcome would probably have followed.

The strongest case should survive those qualifications.
