# July 2017 — Day 2 / Day 4 / Day 8

## Purpose

This page reconstructs the July 2017 admission in temporal terms. It does not assume that any decision was negligent. It separates the principal choices so that each can be tested on the information available when it was made.

The useful sequence is simple:

> **Day 2 — what treatment would not yet be used**  
> **Day 4 — where the unresolved feet would be managed**  
> **Day 8 — whether that was enough to discharge the patient**

---

## Entry — one unresolved presentation

The urgent GP referral did not present neatly separated neurological and podiatric problems. It placed abnormal gait, bilateral foot problems, muscle tension, difficulty initiating movement, increased tone, toe clawing, brisk reflexes and progressive functional deterioration into one open neurological question: `CLIN-000847–CLIN-000884`.

Neurology then found a striking Parkinsonian syndrome, including rigidity, gross bradykinesia, abnormal gait and lower-limb posture described in dystonic terms. Genuine structural foot pathology was also present.

The findings did not require a binary choice between “structural” and “neurological.” Both could coexist.

---

## Day 2 — treatment policy

The surviving account shows that a cautious policy toward conventional dopamine replacement formed early in the admission: `CLIN-001153–CLIN-001180`, `CLIN-001228–CLIN-001229`.

The rationale was the patient's exceptional youth and concern about long-term treatment complications. Levodopa or dopamine agonists were not regarded as irrelevant. The record contemplated that levodopa might work very well and that it would probably be required sooner or later. It also preserved the possibility of future regret from delay.

The patient separately remembers a bedside conversation after the complex case meeting in which the treating neurologist said that his senior had told him not to use the “good stuff”, by which the patient understood levodopa, and warned that the decision might later be regretted. When the patient asked what that meant, he recalls the neurologist saying that he did not want him returning in seven or eight years saying that his life could have been so much better.

### What is fact and what is recollection

**Documentary fact:** cautious dopaminergic policy, anticipated benefit, anticipated future regret.

**Patient recollection:** the precise bedside wording, reference to the senior, “good stuff”, and the seven-or-eight-year explanation.

### Question for assessment

Was the treatment strategy reasonable in July 2017, and if so what prospective rule should have determined when caution had gone on long enough and the policy required reconsideration?

---

## Day 4 — Podiatry becomes the practical route

By 6 July the feet had acquired a practical destination. The GP recorded contact with Podiatry and that the case would be prioritised “as Parkinson's”. The neurological plan also contemplated outpatient chiropody/Podiatry.

Yet the eventual neurological account did not establish that the feet were independent of the neurological syndrome. The relationship was recorded as **“Not clear.”**

This distinction matters:

> **The evidential relationship remained unresolved while the management pathways became more definite.**

The patient was not new to foot care. The treating neurologist's own history recorded chiropody/Podiatry treatment for the foot problem since **2009–10**. That is documentary fact.

The patient's own interpretation is that years of treatment meant he already understood what local foot care could and could not do for him. His later challenge was therefore not, in his account, a complaint that Podiatry had been forgotten. It was a challenge to whether Neurology had sufficiently answered the relationship between the longstanding foot problem and the neurological disorder it had just identified.

### Question for assessment

Was parallel Neurology/Podiatry management reasonable while the relationship remained unresolved? If so, what neurological follow-up or reassessment criteria were required to prevent an unresolved proposition from becoming a permanently separated pathway by default?

---

## Day 8 — challenge to sufficiency of discharge

The patient gives a specific account of a bedside teaching encounter on or around 10 July, shortly before discharge:

- four medical students were present;
- he raised that the disabling foot problem had not been adequately addressed;
- one student went to obtain the neurologist;
- the neurologist returned;
- the patient used a distinctive NCT / bald-tyres analogy; and
- the practical response, as remembered by the patient, directed the feet toward Podiatry.

This remains a **patient-sourced account requiring independent corroboration**.

If corroborated, its significance is not that the patient's own medical theory becomes correct. It is that the adequacy of closure was expressly put before the treating neurologist before the admission ended.

The clinical question then becomes:

> **Given the serious neurological presentation, the expressly unresolved foot relationship, the management choices already made and the patient's stated concern that the central foot problem remained inadequately addressed, was it reasonable to proceed with discharge without further integrated reassessment; and if so, what findings or safeguards made discharge appropriate?**

---

## The MRI comparator

The treating neurologist later said he had been keen to keep the patient in hospital until MRI could be obtained. The native material dates the MRI brain/cervical spine exam and report to **10 July 2017**.

This is important because it demonstrates that the treating clinician himself recognised a concept of **sufficient investigation before discharge**.

The exact order between MRI review, the bedside encounter and finalisation of the discharge decision still requires evidential reconstruction. It should not be assumed.

The potential collision is nevertheless clear:

- a clinician-defined investigative threshold may have been reached; while
- the patient says he raised a different sufficiency question: the feet still had not been integrated into the neurological explanation.

---

## The discharge account

The discharge correspondence was unusually detailed and expressly patient-facing. It was intended to consolidate discussions, help with debriefing and provide a clear plan.

It records the patient as highly intelligent and insightful, able to carry information forward, capable of questioning appropriately and having a **“good understanding”** of why dopaminergic treatment was being delayed.

Understanding does not prove agreement.

The patient does not dispute that he understood the explanation. His account is that he used that understanding to challenge whether the unresolved presenting problem made discharge sufficient.

The reported challenge is not identifiable in the extensive final account.

The narrow documentary issue is therefore:

> **The record preserves the patient's capacity to understand and question, but not the reported question that challenged the sufficiency of closure.**

---

## The document remained capable of later updating

Physical discharge occurred on 11 July. The DaTscan was performed on 13 July and was abnormal. That result was added to the specialist correspondence after discharge before the completed package was received downstream.

The scan strengthened the Parkinsonism proposition. It did not determine whether the feet were structural, neuropathic, dystonic, OFF-related or mixed, and it did not decide whether the Podiatry/Neurology allocation was sufficient.

This creates a precise document-lifecycle question:

> **Between clinical decision and final transmission, what information was allowed to update the authoritative account, and what other material information—if the bedside challenge is established—remained absent?**

No inference of deliberate omission should be drawn without version history, dictation/transcription data and witness evidence.

---

## Why the three days matter

Separating the admission into Day 2, Day 4 and Day 8 prevents it from being treated as one undifferentiated clinical judgment.

The answers may differ at each point.

The original treatment strategy may have been reasonable.

The Podiatry route may have been reasonable.

Discharge may also have been reasonable.

But each proposition has to be justified on its own facts and under the standard that applied at the time.

That is the purpose of this chronology.
